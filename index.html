How to use this

For students: one topic at a time

  1. Open a topic and read what the syllabus asks first.
  2. Read the notes, then close them and say the topic out loud or write it down from memory.
  3. Name your case study for the topic, with a place, a figure and a date.
  4. Answer the self-check questions, aiming for the marks shown, then mark the topic learning or confident.

For mentors: a 40-minute session

  1. 5 min: start with the "Open with" question. Let them talk before you correct anything.
  2. 10 min: go through the "Check they can" list. Spend longer on anything they get stuck on.
  3. 15 min: one timed exam question.
  4. 10 min: mark it together, then agree on their status. Be honest!

Your papers

You sit Paper 1, Paper 2 and Paper 4 (Alternative to Coursework). Grades A* to G.

45%

Paper 1: Geographical Themes

75 marks weighted to 100. Answer three questions, one from each theme, 25 marks each. Each question ends with a 7-mark question on a named example, marked in levels: for 6 or 7 marks you need developed points with details from the place.

1 h 45 min · externally assessed
27.5%

Paper 2: Geographical Skills

60 marks, all questions compulsory. A third of the marks come from the 1:25 000 or 1:50 000 map question. You don't need to know any case studies.

1 h 30 min · pencil, ruler, protractor, calculator
27.5%

Paper 4: Alternative to Coursework

60 marks, all compulsory. Written fieldwork tasks: planning an investigation, collecting and presenting data, testing a hypothesis, and drawing conclusions.

1 h 30 min · externally assessed
Paper 2

Map and graph skills

Answers to the mapwork question must come from map evidence only. Work through this list with a real 1:50 000 extract, not from memory.

On the map extract
  • Four- and six-figure grid references. The first tenth is 0 and the last is 9.
  • Direction as a 16-point compass bearing and as a bearing from grid north (bring a protractor).
  • Distance with a straight paper edge against the scale line; break curves into straight sections.
  • Height difference by contour reading; interpret or complete a cross-section.
  • Describe relief and drainage: ridge, scarp, plateau, flood plain; broad, steep-sided, gently sloping.
  • Settlement pattern (dispersed, nucleated, linear), plus site, growth and function.
  • Communications: type and density in relation to relief.
Graphs and data you must read
  • Population pyramids: compare male/female, working and dependent groups.
  • Line, bar, divided bar, histogram, pictogram, pie.
  • Scatter graphs with best-fit lines; positive and negative relationships.
  • Triangular graphs for employment structure.
  • Wind-rose, radial, kite, dispersion graphs.
  • Isoline maps and choropleth maps.
  • Plot onto given axes accurately, and suggest a suitable graph for a data set.
